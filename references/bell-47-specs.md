# Bell 47 — Technical Specifications and Flying Reference

The Bell 47 is a single-engine, two-blade teetering-rotor light helicopter designed by Arthur M. Young.
First flown 8 December 1945. First helicopter in the world to receive civil certification (8 March 1946).
Produced continuously until 14 February 1974 — over 5,600 built by Bell alone, plus licensed production
by Agusta (Italy), Kawasaki (Japan), and Westland (UK).

The definitive variant is the **47G-3B**, the specifications below unless otherwise noted.

---

## Rotor System

- Configuration: two-blade teetering (semi-rigid) main rotor; two-blade tail rotor
- Main rotor diameter: 11.32 m / 37 ft 2 in (47G-3B)
- Tail rotor diameter: 1.87 m / 6 ft 2 in
- Rotor direction: counter-clockwise viewed from above (US standard)
- Reduction ratio: 9:1 (engine RPM ÷ 9 ≈ rotor RPM)

### The Stabilizer Bar (Flybar)

Arthur Young's key invention: a weighted gyroscopic bar mounted perpendicular to the rotor blades,
spinning at rotor RPM. Bob weights (~4 lb) at each tip give it gyroscopic inertia. The bar
mechanically damps cyclic inputs and absorbs gusts, acting as a low-pass filter between the pilot
and the rotor disc. This made the Bell 47 the first helicopter practical for general civilian use.

The stabilizer bar creates a ~0.7-second lag between cyclic input and aircraft response. Pilots
must anticipate movements rather than react to them.

**Yellow arc on tachometer:** A resonance frequency band where the stabilizer bar can "hunt,"
damaging the bar frame splines. Never operate in the yellow arc.

## The Four Controls

### 1. Collective (left hand)

Pivoting lever to the left of the pilot seat. Raising it increases pitch on all rotor blades
simultaneously, increasing lift and drag. The motorcycle-style throttle twist grip is at the
forward end of the collective.

### 2. Throttle (left hand — twist grip on collective)

**Critical difference from modern turbine helicopters: there is no automatic throttle
correlation on the Bell 47.** The pilot must manually coordinate throttle with every collective
movement to maintain rotor RPM in the green arc (322–370 rpm).

- Collective up → twist throttle outboard (increase RPM) to compensate for increased drag
- Collective down → ease throttle inboard to prevent RPM overspeed

Failure to add throttle when pulling collective = RPM decay.
Failure to reduce throttle when lowering collective = RPM overspeed.

This manual coordination is the most significant challenge for new Bell 47 pilots.

### 3. Cyclic (right hand)

Control stick between the pilot's knees. Tilts the rotor disc via the swashplate.

- Forward → nose down, forward flight
- Aft → nose up, decelerate
- Left/right → bank

Use fingertip pressure only. Heavy-handed inputs will chase the helicopter. Anticipate due to
the ~0.7-second stabilizer bar lag.

### 4. Anti-torque Pedals (feet)

Main rotor turns counter-clockwise (viewed from above), creating torque that yaws the nose
right. Left pedal increases tail rotor pitch, counteracting this.

- More power (higher collective) → more torque → more left pedal
- Less power → ease off left pedal

## Hover

Hovering is the most demanding maneuver in the Bell 47 — described as "standing atop a greased
beach ball." It is learned last, after in-flight control familiarization.

1. Head into wind. Collective full down. Cyclic neutral.
2. Increase throttle to near 370 rpm.
3. Raise collective slowly while simultaneously twisting throttle outboard to maintain RPM.
4. As helicopter becomes light on skids, add left pedal to counteract torque.
5. Make small cyclic corrections to maintain level attitude.
6. Continue raising collective until skids clear; adjust throttle to stay in green arc.
7. Stabilize at 3–5 ft AGL. All four controls require small, coordinated, continuous inputs.

Key: corrections must be small, smooth, and anticipatory. Any correction on one control almost
always requires a coordinated correction on at least one other.

---

## Normal Takeoff

1. Establish stable hover at 3–5 ft AGL into wind.
2. Apply small forward cyclic to begin forward movement.
3. Simultaneously increase collective slightly (and throttle to match) to maintain altitude.
4. As airspeed builds through effective translational lift (ETL, ~16–24 kt), the helicopter
   surges forward and wants to climb — take advantage of this.
5. Establish climb attitude. Best climb speed: ~40–50 mph.

---

## Effective Translational Lift (ETL)

At ~16–24 kt forward speed, the rotor exits its own downwash and enters clean air. Rotor
efficiency increases dramatically, requiring less power for the same lift. The helicopter
surges and wants to climb. The nose may pitch up slightly. Anticipate this with collective
and cyclic adjustments.

---

## Normal Approach and Landing

1. Establish approach at ~10° descent angle, 60–70 kt.
2. Lower collective to initiate descent; adjust throttle for correct RPM.
3. Coordinate cyclic to maintain airspeed; pedals maintain heading.
4. Gradually slow as altitude decreases.
5. At 10–15 ft AGL, transition to hover.
6. At 3–5 ft hover, lower collective smoothly to touch down.
7. On ground: reduce throttle, hold heading with pedals until stopped, then shut down.

**Running landing:** Used when insufficient power to hover (high density altitude or heavy
load). Approach low to the surface, slow to safe taxi speed, roll on skids. Requires a
clear, level surface.

---

## Emergency: Engine Failure / Autorotation

**Immediate actions:**

1. **Lower collective IMMEDIATELY to full down.** This is the single most critical action.
   Rotor RPM decays within seconds at high pitch with no engine power. Below 322 rpm can
   become unrecoverable.
2. The freewheeling (sprag) clutch automatically disconnects the engine from the rotor when
   engine RPM falls below rotor RPM — the rotor continues spinning, driven by upward airflow.
3. Add right pedal (torque is gone; without correction the nose yaws left).
4. Apply aft cyclic to establish autorotation airspeed (~60 kt).
5. Reduce throttle to confirm needle split on the tachometer.
6. Select a landing site.

**Autorotation descent:** ~1,700 ft/min at 60 kt. The Bell 47's high-inertia rotor (heavy blades
with tip weights) stores significant kinetic energy, giving more time than low-inertia types.

**Flare and touchdown:**
1. At ~50 ft AGL, initiate flare with aft cyclic — slows forward speed, builds rotor RPM.
2. At ~15 ft AGL, begin leveling the flare.
3. At ~10 ft AGL, raise collective to arrest rate of descent and cushion touchdown.
4. Lower collective on touchdown.

**Engine failure from hover (5–10 ft AGL):**
Apply right pedal, level attitude with cyclic, use collective to cushion touchdown. Time
measured in seconds — no opportunity to establish autorotation airspeed.

---

## Emergency: Tail Rotor Failure in Forward Flight

Indications: bang, then progressive yaw right as tail rotor stops.

1. Enter autorotation to eliminate torque.
2. Maintain minimum 50 kt to use vertical fin for partial yaw control.
3. Autorotative touchdown at 50 kt, or running landing with power on.

---

## Emergency: Tail Rotor Failure in Hover

Indications: bang, rapid yaw right.

1. Roll throttle off immediately to prevent yaw rate increasing.
2. Use cyclic to hold position.
3. Hovering autorotation — drop straight down.

---

## Emergency: Loss of Tail Rotor Control (Pitch Control Failure)

1. Keep airspeed high — no immediate rush to land.
2. Approach at 35 kt (gives ~30° nose-left yaw).
3. Cross the landing area threshold at 10 ft AGL at 35 kt.
4. Reduce forward speed while managing throttle to align nose before touchdown.
   (Throttle on yaws nose right; throttle off yaws nose left.)
5. Avoid tailwind. Right-front wind is worst; left-front is best approach wind.

---

## Emergency: Vortex Ring State (Settling with Power)

**Trigger:** Descending vertically or at very low forward speed into your own rotor wash, at
moderate power, at more than ~300 ft/min sink rate.

**Indications:** Buffeting and shuddering; loss of lift.

**Recovery:** Lower collective, lower nose, establish forward flight to exit the recirculating
air. Do NOT simply add more power — this makes it worse.

**Prevention:** Never exceed 300 ft/min sink rate at airspeeds below ETL unless in autorotation.

---

## Emergency: Cooling Fan Belt Failure (Turbocharged Models)

Indications: bang or noise from behind cabin; rapid rise in CHT and oil temp.

Land immediately. The engine can seize within 60 seconds of continued powered operation.

## Notable History

- **World's first certified civilian helicopter** (8 March 1946)
- **Korean War medevac:** H-13 Sioux transported ~18,000 of the war's 23,000 total casualties.
  Reduced battlefield death rate from 4.5% (WWII) to 2.5%.
- **M\*A\*S\*H:** The opening credits of all 251+ episodes feature Bell H-13s flying litter
  patients on external skid-mounted stretchers. One aircraft appeared in over 263 episodes.
- **Presidential helicopter:** President Eisenhower became the first U.S. president to fly in a
  helicopter on 12 July 1957, in an Air Force H-13J.
- **First TV news helicopter:** The KTLA "Telecopter" (Los Angeles), 4 July 1958.
- **Apollo astronaut training:** NASA used Bell 47s to familiarize astronauts with autorotation
  sink rates as a simulation for Lunar Module descent.
- **Total production:** ~5,600–6,400 worldwide across Bell, Agusta, Kawasaki, and Westland.
  Production ran from 1946 to 1974 — 28 years.

The Bell 47's distinctive bubble canopy, exposed tubular-steel tailboom, and clop-clop rotor
sound make it one of the most recognizable aircraft ever built.
